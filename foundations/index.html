Foundations in Vancouver

Knowledgeable. Thorough. Resourceful.

LEARN MORE

Foundation design in Vancouver demands a rigorous understanding of the region's complex glacial and post-glacial geology, which commonly includes variable deposits of till, marine silts, and liquefiable sands. A reliable project begins with a thorough geotechnical investigation to characterize these subsurface conditions, directly informing the selection between shallow and deep foundation systems. This critical phase is governed by the BC Building Code, which references national standards such as the National Building Code of Canada and CSA S500 for thermosyphon foundations in permafrost, though Vancouver's temperate climate shifts the focus to seismic resilience and bearing capacity.

Our methodology adheres to the Canadian Foundation Engineering Manual and ASTM standards to mitigate the primary risks of settlement and seismic liquefaction. In-Situ is central to this process, with the Cone Penetration Test (CPT) providing a continuous profile of soil behavior, particularly effective in identifying the loose, saturated sands prone to earthquake-induced failure. These findings are complemented by direct In-Situ methods and precise laboratory analysis of undisturbed samples, where fundamental index tests like grain size analysis (sieve and hydrometer) and Atterberg limits are essential for classifying fine-grained soils and predicting their compressibility.

Typical Vancouver projects, from single-family homes on sloping terrain to high-rise towers in the dense urban core, present distinct challenges that demand tailored foundation solutions. For residential structures on granular soils, we often verify engineered fill compaction through a field density test (sand cone method) to ensure the required bearing capacity for conventional footings. More complex commercial developments frequently require deep foundations, such as driven steel piles or drilled shafts, where CPT data is indispensable for assessing pile driveability and estimating skin friction and end-bearing resistance to resist both static and dynamic lateral loads.

Our process integrates seamless site investigation with clear, actionable reporting. Following the field and laboratory program, we deliver a comprehensive geotechnical report containing design parameters for bearing capacity, settlement estimates, and specific foundation recommendations. This deliverable provides the essential data engineers need to design cost-effective, code-compliant foundations with confidence, effectively de-risking construction by eliminating the uncertainty inherent in Vancouver's challenging subsurface environment.

Quick answers

What is the difference between an active and a passive anchor for a Vancouver excavation?

An active anchor is stressed immediately after grout curing to apply a pre-compressive force to the wall, limiting initial deflection—critical when shoring next to a heritage building in Gastown. A passive anchor, often a fully grouted bar, is not stressed until the soil mass begins to move; it is better suited for temporary cuts where minor deformation is acceptable. In Vancouver's stiff till, active strand anchors allow for tighter deflection tolerances under the NBCC serviceability limits.

How much does anchor design and testing cost for a typical Vancouver site?

For a standard program including design review and two sacrificial proof tests, budgets typically range from CA$1,470 to CA$5,440, depending on whether you need a simple bar anchor verification in till or a comprehensive strand anchor program with extended creep monitoring in the marine clays. Mobilization costs within the Metro Vancouver area are factored into this range, but difficult access sites on the North Shore may require additional logistics.

Can you test anchors in the rain during a Vancouver winter?

Yes, the hydraulic jack and load cell systems are fully sealed for operation in heavy precipitation, which is a standard requirement for fieldwork from November through March in the Lower Mainland. The main concern during winter testing is not the rain itself but the temperature effect on the hydraulic oil viscosity; we allow a warm-up cycle for the power pack and record the ambient temperature to correct the pressure transducer readings if needed.
