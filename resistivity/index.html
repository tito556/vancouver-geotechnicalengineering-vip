Electrical Resistivity Testing (VES) in Vancouver for Subsurface Site Characterization

Knowledgeable. Thorough. Resourceful.

LEARN MORE

The soil profile beneath a project in Kitsilano looks nothing like what you'll find out in Richmond. One sits on dense Vashon till over bedrock. The other floats on hundreds of meters of Fraser River deltaic silts and clays. Relying on a handful of boreholes to connect the dots between two points fifty meters apart is a gamble. Electrical resistivity testing — Vertical Electrical Sounding in particular — fills that gap. It uses current injection to build a continuous 2D image of the subsurface. You see where the clay lenses pinch out. You see where the water table actually sits. For a city where every square meter of land comes at a premium, that kind of data pays for itself before the first excavator arrives. A CPT test can add cone resistance logs to calibrate the resistivity boundaries where access is tight.

A VES survey costs less than a single day of unexpected dewatering on a deep excavation in Vancouver — and it helps you avoid both.

Process overview

In Vancouver, the biggest surprise is usually a buried channel filled with soft organic silt that nobody's test pit hit. We see it on resistivity profiles all the time — a low-resistivity band cutting through higher-resistance till, invisible to standard drilling on a 30-meter grid. The method measures apparent resistivity at expanding electrode spacings. Each spacing pushes the current deeper. A Schlumberger array gives you clean vertical resolution through the glacial sequence. A Wenner array handles lateral changes better when you suspect the bedrock surface is irregular. We run the raw data through iterative inversion software to get true layer resistivities and thicknesses. Then we tie those geo-electric layers to actual soil types using local calibration from an SPT drilling program nearby. The result is a defensible ground model that holds up under geotechnical review.

Local context

The classic Vancouver mistake is assuming bedrock depth is uniform between two boreholes spaced 50 meters apart along a proposed foundation line. We've seen projects where the till-bedrock contact dropped six meters over a distance of twenty. The contractor hit basalt where they planned a mat footing and had to redesign on the fly. Electrical resistivity surveying eliminates that blind spot. It maps the contact continuously. For excavations near the Fraser River or False Creek, the second mistake is misjudging groundwater pathways. A resistivity profile can reveal a high-permeability sand lens that daylight borings missed. If you don't seal that lens during a deep excavation, you're pumping water for the duration of the job. The NBCC and Vancouver Building Bylaw both require a geotechnical investigation that adequately characterizes the site. A resistivity survey is the most cost-effective way to prove you've done that across the entire footprint.

Quick answers

How much does a VES resistivity survey cost for a Vancouver site?

Survey pricing in the Lower Mainland typically ranges from CA$790 to CA$1,400 depending on the number of soundings, profile line length, and site access conditions. A single VES sounding for a house lot runs at the lower end. A full-day 2D imaging profile with multiple lines and inversion processing falls at the upper end. Every quote includes data processing, a signed report with iso-resistivity sections, and correlation to available borehole logs.

Can you do resistivity testing on a paved or urban site in Vancouver?

Yes, though it requires some adaptation. We use bentonite mud coupling on asphalt or concrete surfaces rather than driving stakes. In downtown Vancouver or along busy corridors like Broadway, we schedule surveys in low-traffic windows and work with hand-portable equipment to avoid lane closures. The data quality is slightly reduced compared to direct soil contact, but with inversion constraints from nearby boreholes we still get usable stratigraphic models.

How does electrical resistivity compare to MASW for Vancouver's glacial soils?

They measure different physical properties and we often use both together. Resistivity responds to pore water chemistry and clay content. MASW responds to stiffness. In Vancouver's glacial till, resistivity can separate clean sand from clayey till better than shear-wave velocity alone. The MASW method gives you Vs profiles for seismic site class. Resistivity gives you stratigraphy and water conditions. A combined survey costs more upfront but eliminates ambiguity when the geotechnical model matters.
